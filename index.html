When do cereal shortfalls become food crises?

An experiment, not a forecast. I tested seven ideas about where El Niño’s harvest losses turn into national food crises. None of them worked: no idea warned of the crises after the last two El Niños better than simply flagging every country in southern Africa. The map shows each idea; switch between them with the menu.

The question

A strong El Niño is forming; NOAA says it could be the strongest since records began in 1950. Farmers in southern Africa are planting now for their 2027 harvest. El Niño often brings drought there, but not every bad harvest becomes a food crisis. Could public data have warned which ones would? I tried seven ideas, testing each on the last two El Niños as if the map had been drawn before them.

The answer: none of them

No idea beat the simplest rule I could think of: flag every country in southern Africa. That rule caught 10 of the 12 national food crises, with 10 false alarms. Every idea below caught fewer crises, raised more false alarms, or both.

Idea 1: El Niño shrinks harvests

It does, in southern Africa: Zimbabwe, Namibia and South Africa lose about a quarter or more of their harvest in a typical El Niño. But as a warning, harvest losses caught 8 crises with 49 false alarms. Before 2015–16 they were barely better than chance: 3 of the 31 countries flagged had a crisis, against 3 of the 99 others.

Ideas 2 to 7: harvest losses plus one more measure

A second measure can only remove warnings, so the hope was that it would remove the false ones.

Cereal imports, for dependence on world markets: 5 caught, 37 false alarms. Importers include rich countries that can simply buy grain, and the measure dropped Malawi and Zambia, which grow their own maize and tipped anyway.

Income: 7 caught, 26 false alarms.

Extreme poverty: the fewest false alarms, 8, but it missed 7 of the 12 crises.

Human development and life expectancy: 8 caught each, with 25 and 29 false alarms.

Undernourishment: the best of the six, 8 caught with 17 false alarms. Still fewer crises and more false alarms than flagging southern Africa.

Why none of them worked

Twelve crises are too few to tell a good idea from a lucky one. Much of the harvest signal comes from one drought, 1991–92, the worst in southern Africa in decades; without it, the harvest test is not significant. Shocks that coincide with El Niño blur it further: Mozambique’s apparent harvest signal comes from its civil-war years, not the weather.

What I learned

I like to try ideas out visually, but here the most interesting part was the testing. The same idea can be tested in many ways: draw the map as it would have looked before each El Niño, score it against a simple rule, drop one El Niño at a time, move the cut-off. Each test told me something different. The harvest idea was weak before 2015–16 and good before 2023–24, and much of its signal came from a single drought.

On El Niño itself: where it hits harvests hard, the region is the warning. The capacity to cope matters too, since South Africa loses as much of its harvest as its neighbours but can absorb a bad year, yet none of these national measures showed where crises would follow.

Cautions: the map shows associations, not causes or crisis probabilities. National figures hide local failures, cereals miss staples like sweet potato, and the crisis rule counts any weather-driven crisis (in 2016 and 2024, mostly El Niño droughts).

How we made this map

Harvest shortfall. A smooth trend (LOWESS) is fitted to each country’s cereal production since 1975, and El Niño harvests are compared with those of neutral years, with neither El Niño nor La Niña (NOAA); in southern Africa the harvest after the event counts. Production, not yield, because failed fields go unharvested. Countries need 30 years of data and 50,000 tonnes a year. Classes: under 5%, 5–15% and over 15%; hatching marks p of 0.1 or more (Mann-Whitney test). The shortfall measures exposure and sensitivity, not the capacity to cope, so it is not a full picture of vulnerability.

Second measures. The explorer pairs the shortfall with one other measure, in three classes fixed before testing: cereal imports (FAO’s import dependency ratio, the 2020–22 average, recomputed from its food balance sheets; 25% and 50%), income (the World Bank’s income groups, from GDP per person), extreme poverty (25% and 50%), the Human Development Index (0.70 and 0.55), life expectancy (the median, 74 years, and 65) and undernourishment (FAO’s three-year average; 10% and 25%; FAO reports prevalence below 2.5% only as under 2.5%). A country is flagged with a shortfall of 5% or more and the middle or top class. Income, poverty and undernourishment use values from before each crisis period, as estimated today; poverty comes from each country’s latest survey, which for 2023–24 is more than five years old in about a third of countries. Imports, the Human Development Index and life expectancy are single recent values used for both periods, a look-ahead for 2015–16. Regions follow the UN’s M49 grouping, with Sudan in Sub-Saharan Africa.

Notes on the map. The world view’s notes come from the map’s data, except two that rest on research: the Global Report on Food Crises attributes Haiti’s 2016 crisis to drought worsened by El Niño, then Hurricane Matthew, and Timor-Leste’s IPC analysis names El Niño drought first among the drivers of its 2023–24 crisis.

Testing the map. From what was known before each period (El Niños up to 2009 and harvests up to 2014 for 2015–16; up to 2018–19 and 2022 for 2023–24), a country is flagged at a shortfall of 5% or more. 2015–16: 3 of 31 flagged countries had a national food crisis, against 3 of 99 others (Fisher’s exact test, p = 0.15); 2023–24: 5 of 26 against 1 of 106 (p = 0.001). Both rounds together: 8 of 57 against 4 of 205 (p = 0.0008), optimistic because countries appear in both. Counting dashed borders: 5 of 26 against 2 of 106 for 2023–24 (p = 0.003). Without the 1991–92 El Niño, 2023–24 becomes 2 of 25 against 4 of 107 (p = 0.32); dropping any other single El Niño leaves it significant. At a 3% cut-off it holds (6 of 41 against 0 of 91); at 10% it weakens (p = 0.12). Flagging every southern African country caught 10 of 12 crises with 10 false alarms, the shortfall 8 with 49, and southern African countries with a shortfall 8 with 6.

El Niño years. Past El Niños are dated with NOAA’s Oceanic Niño Index (ONI), the standard until 2026. NOAA now uses the Relative Oceanic Niño Index (RONI), which subtracts the warming of the tropical oceans as a whole; the forecast in the story uses RONI. By RONI only 2014 changes, to neutral, and 2023–24 becomes 5 of 30 against 1 of 102 (p = 0.002). A smoother trend (span 0.75) gives 4 of 33 against 2 of 99 (p = 0.03): the effect holds in every version, but its size depends on these choices.

Borders. Solid: a national food crisis, at least 10% of a country’s population in IPC or Cadre Harmonisé Phase 3 (Crisis) or worse at the peak after an El Niño, in 2016 or 2023/24, with weather extremes as the main driver. Dashed: 5–10% on the same rule, or a joint government–UN emergency appeal for El Niño drought. Figures: the Global Report on Food Crises 2017 and its 2024 and 2025 country profiles, and Timor-Leste’s IPC analysis; South Africa’s 2016 figure is a government estimate the Global Report accepted. Population: World Bank, 2024. Somaliland counts as Somalia.

Not yet verified. El Salvador (2023–24), Bangladesh (2024), Angola (2024) and North Korea (2016) could not be checked against the rule. Each could shift a count by one; none would change the conclusion.
